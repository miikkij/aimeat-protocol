# Tidewater (board game) · playtest 1 · 8 March 2026 · First playtest

We played the first proper session of Tidewater at my place on Sunday afternoon. Grey, sleety day, which felt right for a game about ships. Pauli and Venla came over around one, I made too much coffee, and we spent the first half hour just reading through the rules draft together before anyone touched a piece. That turned out to be useful in itself: half the problems we found today were naming problems rather than rules problems.

## The rules draft and the words we settled on

The draft I printed was written in a hurry and kept calling things by three different names. By the end of the afternoon we'd agreed on a vocabulary, mostly by accident, and I'm writing it down here so the next version of the draft uses it consistently.

- **cargo card**: I'd been writing "goods card," "load," and "freight" interchangeably. Pauli asked which one was the real one, and we went with cargo card: one card is one load of goods a ship carries from one harbour to another. Simple, and it reads well on the card back.
- **harbour tile**: the board tile for one harbour, where cargo gets dropped off and picked up. I'd been calling them "ports" in some places. Venla pointed out that "port" is also left on a ship, and with a nautical theme someone will eventually get confused. Harbour tile it is.
- **hub**: the central harbour tile where all the sea lanes meet. Venla called it "the hub" about ten minutes into play, while pointing at it to ask whether everything really had to go through the middle. Nobody had a better word, so it stuck.
- **fog bank**: board tiles that ordinary ships can't enter. In the draft they were just "blocked tiles," which is accurate and boring. Pauli said "so it's a fog bank," and that was that. "Ordinary" is doing some work there because I have a half-idea about ships that *can* go in, but that's parking lot, not rules.
- **captain sheet**: each player's card showing their ship's hold and its abilities. The draft called it a "player board," but it's a card, not a board, and Venla wants to draw a little portrait of the captain in the corner. So it's a captain sheet.
- **drop**: playing a cargo card face down onto a harbour tile. Pauli started saying "I'll drop this here" on his second turn, and by the third turn all of us were saying it. It's a much better verb than "deliver," because the card goes face down and nobody else knows what it was yet. That hidden bit is the part of the game I'm most pleased with so far.
- **loop**: one full round in which every player has had a turn. I needed a word for it while explaining when the whales move ("at the end of each... round? cycle?"). I said loop, mostly because the turn order goes around the table, and it was short enough to survive.
- **pod**: a group of three whale tokens that moves as one piece and blocks a sea lane. In the draft the whales moved separately, which was fiddly and slow. Venla had drawn the whale tokens as a little family, and when we were moving them one by one she said "can't the pod just move together?" We tried it for the second half of the game and it was immediately better. So: three whale tokens, one piece, and wherever the pod sits, that sea lane is blocked.
- **dawn run**: see below.

## The dawn run

This was Pauli's idea, and it came out of frustration. The first turn of the game was dead: everyone started at their home harbour tile with nothing to do except move one space toward the hub, and the first loop was basically three people shuffling ships forward. Pauli said the opening felt like "everyone rowing out at dawn," and suggested we just let everyone move twice on the first turn to get going. Venla named it the dawn run, and we played the restart with it.

So for now, the dawn run is the first turn of the game, and every player moves their ship twice. It definitely helped. Ships reached the interesting parts of the board by the second loop instead of the fourth, and the first drop happened much earlier. But I'm not treating it as settled. Pauli wondered whether the player who goes last should get something extra, since by then the good lanes near the hub are already taken. Venla wondered whether you should be allowed to drop during the dawn run or only move. We didn't resolve either. I want to see it with different numbers of players before I touch it.

## Player count

We had a fairly long discussion about this over the second pot of coffee. **Decision: Tidewater is for 2 to 4 players.**

Reasons, as we talked them through:

- The hub gets crowded fast. Even with three of us, there were turns where all three ships were within a lane or two of it, and with the pod parked on one of the lanes it got tight. More ships than four and I think the middle of the board just jams.
- The hidden drop only works if you can roughly keep track of what the others might have dropped. Venla said that with three opponents she'd already be guessing; past that it would turn into pure luck.
- Two players should work, because the pod and the fog banks give you something to play against even without a crowd. Pauli was sceptical. He thinks a two-player game might feel empty on this board. That's fair, and we haven't actually tried it. But I'd rather design for two and find out than leave it out.
- I want the box to say something that fits most of the groups I actually play with, and 2 to 4 does.

None of us has played a two- or four-player game yet, so this is the range we're designing for, not one we've proved.

## Game length

**Decision: a game should take about 45 minutes.**

Today's game took far longer than that, partly because we kept stopping to argue about words and partly because the restart doubled it. Even so, it was obvious it's too long as written. The middle got repetitive: pick up, sail, drop, pick up, sail, drop.

Why 45 minutes:

- Venla said the games she actually gets to the table on weeknights are the ones you can finish before people start looking at their phones. Something around 45 minutes can be played twice in an evening, or once after dinner without anyone checking the time.
- Pauli pointed out the theme doesn't support an epic. It's a trading game with some blocking, not a campaign. Let it be tight.
- For me, it's a target to design toward. If it runs long, I cut loops or cargo, not ideas.

How we actually get there is open. Options we talked about and left alone: fewer cargo cards in the deck, an end trigger tied to the number of drops at the hub, or a fixed number of loops. I don't want to pick one before I've timed a cleaner game.

## Other bits

- The fog banks near the hub were placed badly. One of them sealed off a harbour tile almost completely, and Pauli's ship spent several loops sailing the long way round. I'll move it before next time. That's a layout fix, not a rule.
- Venla wants the captain sheets to be a bit bigger so the hold is readable from across the table. She also sketched a whale on the back of my rules draft, which is now the best thing in the rules draft.
- Pauli's phone rang twice during the restart (his sister, something about a car). We waited both times. That has nothing to do with the game, but it explains why the timing today means nothing.
- Parking lot: ships that can enter fog banks; whether the pod should drift on its own at the end of a loop or only move when someone pushes it; a scoring bonus for dropping at the hub. None of this is decided, just written down so I don't lose it.

Overall: better than I feared. The drop works, the pod works once it moves as one piece, and the dawn run fixed a dead opening. The middle is too long and the board layout needs work.
