## The honesty shelf

This was the first red list item, and the one Ilona had been putting off because she knew what the numbers would say.

Recap, for my own memory: on 10 March, the same day as first drop, we decided to try an honesty shelf on the Alder Court pod. The reasoning was sound at the time. Growers had produce that didn't fit the box sheets and would otherwise go to compost. A shelf on top of the pod with pay-by-phone seemed like an easy way to turn that into a bit of money for the growers. It would also give non-members a reason to stop at the pod, and it would tell us something about how far a lobby trusts itself. Raimo was fine with it as long as it stayed tidy, and to his credit it mostly did.

Ilona went through ten weeks of what went up on the shelf and matched it against the phone payments. About 40 % of the surplus on it was never paid for. It wasn't one bad week dragging the average down either. Some weeks were better and some worse, but none came anywhere near what we'd hoped. The produce wasn't rotting on the shelf; it was disappearing, just without money following it.

Ilona called it "shrink", which she says is the shop word for anything that walks out the door or spoils before it sells. We were both using it within ten minutes and I expect it'll stay. For the shelf, nearly all the shrink is unpaid taking. Spoilage barely features.

That raised the obvious question, and I wrote it down so we wouldn't skip it: can the honesty shelf be protected from unpaid takings? We went through the options properly:

- **A better sign.** Neither of us believes a sign moves 40 %.
- **A camera over the pod.** Dropped almost at once. The HA would have to agree. Filming a lobby that residents walk through every day is not something we want to be associated with. It would also turn the pod into a shop with a guard, which is the opposite of the whole idea.
- **Putting the surplus behind a door**, so a compartment opens once someone has paid. Then it isn't an honesty shelf any more; it's a tiny vending machine. Every compartment holding surplus is a compartment not holding a box, and a K-12 only has twelve.
- **Only stocking it when someone is around.** Nobody is around. That's the point of a pod.
- **Taking payment at the pod before anything can be taken.** This touches the open question of card payment at the pod itself, and we didn't want to settle that sideways through the shelf. Left alone; it's still open.

Ilona's view was that this isn't a design flaw we can patch. A lobby just doesn't behave like a farm gate. People who would never take a box from a compartment will apparently take a bunch of carrots off a shelf "and pay later", and later doesn't come.

My worry was partly the growers: that 40 % is their produce, effectively given away under our name. Mostly, though, it was the theft angle. The pods hold members' boxes. Anything that teaches a lobby that things on the pod are up for grabs is bad for the pod as a whole, even if the shelf and the compartments are technically separate. I would rather not run the experiment of finding out whether one leads to the other.

**Decision: the honesty shelf is dropped. About 40 % of the surplus on it was never paid for.** This replaces the decision of 10 March to try an honesty shelf on the Alder Court pod. The original reasons weren't wrong. The surplus problem is real and growers still have produce that doesn't fit the box sheets. But the shelf doesn't solve it. It loses about four out of every ten things put on it, and it puts the idea of free-for-all produce right on top of the pod.

That also settles the question raised earlier in the session. Can the honesty shelf be protected from unpaid takings? No, not in any way that keeps it an honesty shelf. So it's dropped.

What happens to surplus now is **not** decided. A few half-ideas went on the parking lot:

- Offering surplus as an extra in Picker that members could add to their box.
- Sending surplus to the Harbour Pantry along with the OBs on the orphan run. This would need Hilda's agreement, and Ilona isn't sure they want loose produce rather than boxes.
- Holding a bit back to fill gaps in a short week. I said that sounds tidier on paper than it would be on a Monday night.

None of these were discussed far enough to mean anything.

Practical side: the shelf comes off the pod. Ilona will let Raimo know first, as a courtesy since he's been patient with it. I'll take the payment link down. Alder Court's building champion should hear before the shelf disappears, so nobody thinks it was stolen, which would be a very on-brand end for it. A short member note to Alder Court will follow.

## Wake codes, the QR stub, and sticky doors

The second red list item was how members open their compartments. Once you start counting shrink, you start looking at everything.

Recap: on 3 March, before first drop, we decided that a member opens their compartment with a 6-digit wake code sent by SMS. The reasons were good then too. It works on any phone, it needs no login at the pod, and it was simple to build: the Ledger sends the code, the member types it on the spine's screen, and the Larder layer turns it into a door pulse. For a pilot that needed to get off the ground in a week, that was right.

Two things have changed since.

**First, the theft angle.** A wake code is six digits in a text message. It can be forwarded, read off a lock screen, or written on a sticky note by the door. Oskar at Harbour Row (our building champion there, the resident, not Oskar at Kestrel) told Ilona last week that some of his neighbours forward their codes to whoever happens to be home. That isn't a problem in itself; a partner picking up a box is fine and the whole point. But it does mean the code isn't tied to the member in any real way. We have no proof of a box going missing because of a forwarded code. After the shelf, though, I'm done assuming good behaviour will cover for loose design.

**Second, the SMS itself.** The codes all go out in one burst after the dawn run, and some arrive late. Ilona says a good share of the messages that reach her phone are some version of "where's my code?" Others are about mistyping on the spine screen, or trying last week's code. A few SP members basically never open Picker. They paid upfront and live entirely off the text message, which is fine until the text is late.

My proposal was to show a QR code in Picker, on the member's box page, that they hold up to the spine. The spine reads it, the Ledger checks it, and the door pulse goes out. Ilona kept calling it "the QR thing". I called it a stub because it works like the tear-off part of a ticket: one box, one drop, and once it's used, it's done. Ilona put the two together, "QR stub", and that's what it's called now.

Why it's better than the wake code as the main route:

- **It's tied to the member's Picker login.** Letting someone else pick up means handing over your phone or your account, which is a deliberate act rather than a forward-and-forget.
- **There's no SMS lag.** The stub is there in Picker as soon as the box is in.
- **It ties into the drop scan.** The stub can sit alongside the drop scan record from Field. If a member says their compartment was empty, we can see when the box went in and how the door was opened.
- **No typing on the spine screen**, so no mistyped codes.

Ilona pushed hard on keeping the wake code, and she's right. Phones die. Some lobbies have poor signal. Some members don't want to log into anything at seven in the morning, and the SP members mentioned above would be stranded without it. So the wake code stays, as the fallback.

**Decision: the QR stub in Picker becomes the main way to open a compartment; the wake code stays as a fallback.** This replaces the decision of 3 March that a member opens their compartment with a 6-digit wake code sent by SMS. The SMS code was the quickest thing that worked for first drop. Now that we're thinking about shrink and who actually opens a door, it's too loose to be the main way in. And the late texts generate more of Ilona's phone traffic than anything else.

While digging through the door pulse logs for all this, I found something else. Some compartment doors don't open on the first door pulse. The spine acknowledges the pulse, the lock doesn't release, and a second try works. I'd been calling them "sticky doors" in my head for a few days and said it out loud. Ilona laughed, then used it twice in the next sentence, so it's stuck (sorry).

It matters here because a sticky door looks to the member exactly like a wrong code. They assume they mistyped, ask for the code again, get annoyed, and message Ilona. With the QR stub I can have Picker notice that the door didn't report open and offer a retry, rather than leaving the member staring at a closed door. That's my plan for how to build it, not something we decided.

What I don't know yet is where the stickiness comes from: the lock itself, the spine, or the timing in the Larder layer. I want to ask Oskar at Kestrel whether sticky doors are a known thing on the K-12. Before I write to him, I'll pull the logs per compartment to see whether they cluster on particular doors or whether chill cells behave differently from dry cells. No point emailing a supplier with "sometimes doors are a bit sticky".
