# Tidewater (board game) · playtest 3 · 31 May 2026 · Trading and scoring

Grey, drizzly Sunday, so nobody minded being stuck indoors all afternoon. Pauli and Venla came over around lunch, Venla brought cinnamon buns, and we set up at the kitchen table with far too much coffee. The plan for today was to finally try the trading idea I've been sitting on since the last session, and to get a first real feel for scoring beyond "whoever delivered the most wins."

Setup went quicker than last time now that the captain sheets are printed instead of handwritten. The dawn run went the way it always seems to: Pauli spent both moves racing for the hub, Venla went wide, and I tried something in between and regretted it. Venla got caught by the reef again in the middle of the game and grumbled about it. We're still not sure whether the reef is too punishing; that stays open, and we didn't try to solve it today.

## Trading

I'd written up a rough version beforehand: after each loop, before anyone takes the next turn, everybody can offer cargo cards to anyone else at the table. Open haggling, no restrictions yet, because I wanted to see what people do when nothing stops them.

Pauli started calling it "the window" almost straight away. The reasoning was that it felt like a shop opening its hatch, with everyone crowding in, and that it closes again: once the next loop starts, no more deals. By the second time round all three of us were saying "window", and I've given up writing "trading phase" in my own notes. So: the window is the trading phase between two loops, when players may trade cargo.

How it played:

- The first window was slow. Nobody knew what a card was worth and we mostly stared at each other.
- By the later windows Venla was running what Pauli called a "flea market", collecting cards nobody else could use and swapping them on for the ones that matched Venla's route.
- Pauli used one window to get rid of a card that couldn't be delivered, because a pod was parked across the only lane to that harbour. This is exactly the kind of interaction I was hoping for. The board creates a problem and the table solves it.
- The bad side: windows take time. Open haggling stretches, and someone always wants "one more offer." Every window added real minutes to the game.

We talked through ways to tighten it: a sand timer on each window, limiting everyone to one trade per window, or only letting players trade when their ships share a harbour tile. None of these were decided. I want a few more games with the loose version first, so I can see whether people speed up naturally once they know the cards.

## Game length

This is where trading forced a decision. Back on 8 March 2026 we decided a game should take about 45 minutes. The thinking then was that Tidewater should be a quick, light game you could set up after dinner and maybe play twice. At that point the game was basically movement and deliveries, and 45 minutes fit that shape.

It doesn't fit anymore. With a window between every loop, plus scoring to keep track of, 45 minutes only works if the window shrinks to almost nothing. Then we'd lose the part that got the most laughs today. Venla argued hardest for keeping trading generous and said it's the first time the game felt like it was about the people at the table and not just the board. Pauli was the reluctant one, because the short game was a big part of the appeal. Pauli agreed on one condition: the new target has to be a real target, not an excuse for creeping past it.

So we're going back on the March decision. **A game may take about 60 minutes.** This replaces "a game should take about 45 minutes." If the windows can't fit inside the hour, the fix is to tighten the windows, not to stretch the hour again. I wrote that in capitals on the rules draft so I can't pretend I forgot.

## Scoring: champion and beacons

Two new things here, neither settled beyond what the words mean.

Halfway through, Venla doodled a little crowned fish on a scrap of card to mark who was in the lead. Pauli announced in a sports-commentator voice that "the champion" had changed hands, and that was that. The champion is now the token held by the player with the most points. It moves whenever someone overtakes. Right now it doesn't do anything except make the leader nervous, which honestly worked better than I expected. Pauli suggested the champion should carry a small drawback so the leader becomes a target, maybe not being allowed to trade in the window. We liked it as an idea but didn't adopt it. We also didn't decide how ties work; today we just left the token where it was.

The other one: during the break Venla sketched a little lighthouse standing on a harbour tile. I'd been calling these "lights" or "towers" in my draft, and neither sounded right. Venla said "beacon", because a beacon is something ships steer by, and it stuck immediately. A beacon is a light a player builds on a harbour tile for points. I used placeholder costs and points today, numbers I made up on the spot just to see it work. I'm not writing them down as rules because we didn't agree on anything. Pauli worried that beacons on the harbours next to the hub would be far too strong, since everyone passes by there. Fair point. I need to look at it before next time.

Venla also half-joked that a beacon should let you see into a fog bank. It went on the parking lot without discussion.

## Orphans

Near the end there were several cargo cards just lying on harbour tiles. In each case someone had done a drop meaning to come back for it, and then a pod moved into the way or the game simply ran out. Pauli pointed at one and said "look at that poor orphan", and everyone laughed. The name is staying: an orphan is a cargo card left on a harbour tile with no ship to carry it.

The problem the orphans showed is that dropping is currently free. There's no downside to a speculative drop, so people scatter cards around "just in case," and the board gets cluttered with stuff nobody intends to finish. We talked about having orphans go back into the deck, or letting other players claim them, but the simplest fix won out.

**Every orphan left at the end costs the player who dropped it one point.**

Reasons:

- It makes a drop a small commitment instead of a free action.
- It rewards planning a route through to the end, not just the next couple of turns.
- It gives the last loops some cleanup tension, with people rushing back to collect their own strays. That should feel good, and it plays nicely into the champion changing hands at the very end.

One practical snag Venla spotted right away: drops are face down, so at the end we couldn't always remember who had dropped which card. Today we sorted it out by memory and some arguing. That won't scale. I need some way to mark ownership, maybe a coloured edge matching the captain sheet, or a tiny marker placed alongside the drop.
