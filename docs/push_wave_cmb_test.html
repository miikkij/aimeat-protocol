The CMB Power Spectrum — The Final Test

This is the hardest test any alternative gravity theory can face. Most die here. Let's see what happens.

What the CMB Power Spectrum Is

380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled enough for atoms to form. Photons stopped bouncing off free electrons and flew straight — becoming the Cosmic Microwave Background. The temperature pattern of those photons is a snapshot of the universe at age 380,000 years.

That snapshot isn't smooth. It has hot spots and cold spots at different angular sizes. When you decompose it by scale, you get the acoustic power spectrum — a curve with specific peaks.

Each peak tells you something specific:

PeakAngular scaleWhat it measures
1st peak (ℓ ≈ 220)~1°Geometry of the universe (flat/curved)
2nd peak (ℓ ≈ 540)~0.4°Baryon density (ratio odd/even peaks)
3rd peak (ℓ ≈ 810)~0.25°Dark matter density — THE KILLER TEST

Why the Third Peak Is the Problem

Before recombination, the universe was a hot plasma of baryons (protons, neutrons) coupled to photons via free electrons. This baryon-photon fluid oscillated: gravity pulled it into overdense regions, photon pressure pushed it out. Like a spring with a weight on it.

In the standard model, Cold Dark Matter (CDM) provides something crucial: gravitational potential wells that don't oscillate. CDM doesn't interact with photons. It just sits there, providing extra gravitational pull. The baryon-photon fluid oscillates IN these CDM wells.

This has a specific observable effect:

Planck data shows the third peak IS nearly as high as the first. This is universally cited as proof that non-baryonic dark matter existed in the early universe.

The Push-Wave Framework's Answer

The Problem, Stated Clearly

In the push-wave framework, there are no dark matter particles. The "dark matter effect" at galactic scales comes from accumulated push-waves of visible stars. But at 380,000 years after the Big Bang, there are no stars yet. No galaxies. Just hot plasma. So what provides the extra gravitational wells?

The Proposed Mechanism: The Primordial Wave Medium

Every particle in the early universe — every proton, neutron, electron — is a push-wave source. These particles are in thermal equilibrium at temperature T, and their push-wave emission creates a primordial wave medium.

Here is the critical question: does this wave medium oscillate with the baryon-photon fluid, or does it behave as a separate, non-oscillating component?

The argument that it does NOT oscillate with the baryons:

1. The wave medium integrates over history. Push-waves propagate outward at c from every particle that has ever existed. The wave medium at any point is the superposition of waves emitted from every particle over the entire 380,000-year history of the universe. A local acoustic oscillation — baryons sloshing back and forth over ~100,000 years — modulates the local emission, but the accumulated medium from 380,000 years of emission is dominated by the time-averaged density, not the instantaneous oscillation.

ρ_wave(x, t) = ∫₀ᵗ ∫ ρ_baryon(x', t') × G_ret(x-x', t-t') d³x' dt'

where G_ret is the retarded Green's function. The time integral smooths out oscillations with periods shorter than the light-crossing time of the relevant region.

2. The wave medium is non-local. The push-wave medium at a given point comes from all directions — from matter that is causally connected but physically distant. An acoustic oscillation in one region doesn't affect the wave medium contribution arriving from a region on the opposite side of the sound horizon. The medium is an average over many incoherent patches.

3. Analogy: reverberant sound field. In a reverberant room, even if the speaker oscillates, the total sound energy density in the room stays roughly constant because the reflected waves from all walls average out the oscillation. The push-wave medium is the "reverberant field" of the primordial plasma.

The Quantitative Challenge

For this to work quantitatively, the wave medium must be approximately 5 times denser than the baryon gravitational contribution alone. In standard cosmology:

Ω_CDM ≈ 0.26   vs   Ω_baryon ≈ 0.05   →   ratio ≈ 5:1

Can baryonic push-waves produce 5× the gravitational effect of the baryons themselves?

The push-wave medium density at a point, from a uniform baryonic plasma of density ρ_b, integrating over the causal horizon (radius c × t, where t = 380,000 years):

ρ_wave = ∫₀ᴿ 4πr² × ρ_b × (A²/r²) dr = 4π × ρ_b × A² × R

where R = c × t is the causal horizon radius and A² is the wave coupling constant. The wave medium density scales linearly with the horizon size. At 380,000 years:

R_horizon ≈ 380,000 light-years ≈ 1.2 × 10¹¹ km

The ratio ρ_wave / ρ_baryon = 4π × A² × R. For this to equal 5:

A² = 5 / (4π × R) ≈ 5 / (4π × 3.6 × 10²² m) ≈ 10⁻²³ m⁻¹

This is a tiny coupling constant, which is physically reasonable — push-wave coupling to gravity is extremely weak (as it must be, since gravity is the weakest force).

The key insight: the wave medium amplification factor grows with the age of the universe. Early universe → small horizon → less amplification. Late universe → large horizon → more amplification. This naturally explains why "dark matter" effects are small in the early universe but dominant today in galaxies (where the medium has accumulated over 13.8 billion years).

The Honest Assessment

What Works

The push-wave framework provides a plausible mechanism for non-oscillating gravitational potential wells in the early universe: the accumulated, time-integrated push-wave medium from 380,000 years of baryonic emission. The medium is smooth because it integrates over history and averages over causally disconnected regions. It grows with the horizon size, which explains why "dark matter" effects scale with cosmic age.

What's Genuinely Hard

Problem 1: The third peak height requires precise tuning. In ΛCDM, the CDM density is a free parameter that's fit to the data. In the push-wave framework, the wave medium strength should be calculable from the baryon density and the coupling constant. If it gives the wrong ratio, the framework fails the CMB test. We don't yet have the calculation precise enough to know.

Problem 2: The damping tail. At high multipoles (ℓ > 1000), the power spectrum falls off due to Silk damping — photon diffusion that washes out small-scale fluctuations. The exact shape of this damping depends on the baryon-to-total-matter ratio. ΛCDM fits it with 6 parameters to extraordinary precision. The push-wave model would need to match this with the wave medium coupling as the replacement parameter. Possible in principle, but unverified.

Problem 3: Polarization. The CMB is polarized. The polarization pattern (E-modes and B-modes) provides independent constraints. ΛCDM predicts the polarization pattern correctly. The push-wave framework hasn't addressed this at all.

Problem 4: Baryon Acoustic Oscillations. The acoustic scale (150 Mpc) is also measured in the distribution of galaxies today. This provides a cross-check between early and late universe physics. ΛCDM passes this cross-check. The push-wave model needs to show that its wave medium produces the same acoustic scale in both the CMB and galaxy distribution.

Where There's a Glimmer

Interestingly, MOND — which shares the push-wave framework's rejection of dark matter particles — successfully predicted the 1st-to-2nd peak ratio (McGaugh 1999) before it was measured. This prediction was confirmed by BOOMERanG, WMAP, and Planck. However, MOND failed to predict the correct third peak height. The third peak came in higher than the no-CDM model predicted.

The push-wave framework might do better than MOND here because it DOES have a non-baryonic gravitational component — the wave medium itself. MOND modifies the force law but has no extra matter-like component. The push-wave medium IS an extra gravitational component, just not made of particles. This is a genuine structural advantage over MOND.

There's also an intriguing note from the research: the Hubble tension — the disagreement between the expansion rate measured from the CMB versus local measurements — may indicate that the standard ΛCDM model's fit to the CMB isn't perfect either. Some physicists argue this tension points to new physics beyond ΛCDM, possibly related to the dark matter sector. If so, the push-wave framework's different handling of "dark matter" might naturally resolve it.

This is a PhD thesis worth of work. Maybe two. It requires modifying a Boltzmann solver (like CAMB or CLASS) to replace the CDM component with a wave medium evolution equation. This is not something that can be done in an afternoon.

For reference: the ΛCDM model took hundreds of physicists decades to develop from "hot Big Bang idea" (1960s) to "precision fit to CMB" (2000s). Asking the push-wave framework to match that in a day is unreasonable. What matters now is whether the mechanism is plausible enough to justify doing the calculation.
